Arya Didehvar

Second year at the McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, class of 2029. Concentrating in finance and management, with a minor in data science.

Most of the work I've liked has come down to checking something. Whether fourteen mining companies were really comparable once you stripped out the currencies they reported in. Whether a seller's description of his own business held up against the contract sitting underneath it.

That part I find interesting rather than tedious, which is most of why I keep ending up in this kind of work. I want to go into investment banking.

Work

Aegis Financial

Summer Analyst, equity research. May to August 2026.

Aegis runs small-cap, deep-value funds. I spent the summer on the mining and energy names.

The main thing I built was a comparables model covering fourteen gold companies the funds already held. Comps are simple when companies file the same way, and these didn't. Some were Australian and reported in Australian dollars, some were Canadian, a few were American, and each one's project economics had been run at whatever gold price happened to be current when its technical report was published. So most of the job was normalization: converting everything into one currency at current FX and re-running the project values at a single spot gold price, so that the numbers I was lining up against each other were actually the same kind of number.

I also limited the set to single-project companies. A company with four mines at four different stages has four risk profiles inside one share price, and I had no defensible way to compare that against a company with one. Narrowing the screen cost me names and made the output mean something.

From there the model carried each company's project NPV, annual production in ounces, price to NAV, and enterprise value per ounce, plus sensitivities on the NPVs. The inputs came from technical reports, which are long and not written for convenience. I built an HTML dashboard on top of the model that one of the full-time analysts used.

The part I learned the most from was not the model. Because the funds held large positions in small companies, the portfolio managers could get executives on the phone, and I got to sit in and listen to how they asked questions. They were not there for the company's pitch. They were hunting for the one assumption that would break the forecast if it turned out to be wrong. That is a different skill from building the thing, and I am not good at it yet.

Frank Batten Investment Fund

Investment Analyst. January 2026 to present.

Batten is UVA's student-run investment fund. I was one of about sixteen admitted out of a pool of more than five hundred. The first semester is a ten-week training program, three hours every Sunday, working through accounting, valuation and DCF mechanics, alongside general body meetings on topics like debt.

Training ends in a project. Mine was a sell-side pitch on Proto Labs, a digital manufacturing company that does quick-turn prototyping and low-volume production runs. The argument was that it should be sold, and that Jabil or Siemens would be the natural buyer. I put the offer at $78.53 a share, a 25% premium, all cash.

It rested on two things I thought the market was reading wrong. The first was margins. Proto Labs' EBITDA margin had fallen from roughly 21% in 2020 to about 11% in 2025, and the reason was a deliberate run of acquisitions to build out a manufacturing partner network. That spending was finished, which makes the compression the end of an investment cycle rather than the beginning of a decline. The second was reshoring. The company is built for exactly the short-run domestic manufacturing that tariffs and supply chain re-domestication push toward, and I did not think that was in the price.

I built the DCF, the comparables and the accretion/dilution analysis and presented it with Q&A. The Q&A taught me more than the deck did, mostly by showing me which parts of my own work I could not defend.

Liber Management

Winter Analyst. December 2025 to January 2026.

Liber is a search fund, so the job is finding a business worth buying rather than analyzing one somebody has already put in front of you.

Day to day that meant sourcing. Each morning there was a call, an hour or two, part training on valuation and what makes a business good, part a briefing on whichever fragmented niche we were working through that day. Then I had a sheet of fifty or sixty companies to get through: confirm the business was real and still operating, find who actually owned it, one to three names, and find a working email for them. These were small family-run companies, the kind with a one-page website and no press coverage, so confirming ownership usually meant assembling it out of filings, licensing records and whatever else was public. Over the internship that came to more than a thousand companies.

One of them became my project for the rest of the internship: a founder-owned compliance services business in a fragmented industry, with an owner past retirement age and no succession in place. I wrote a teaser on it first, then a full acquisition recommendation with an LBO and a returns sensitivity.

The teaser was credulous. I had read the seller's materials as a description of the business when they were also a sales document. Building the full pitch, I found that the company's central selling point, that it could deliver several services together that competitors sold separately, ran through an agreement with an outside supplier. And that agreement restricted where and to whom the company was allowed to sell. The growth story I had written in the teaser, expansion into adjacent customer types, was the specific thing the contract prohibited. Separately, I flagged a projection in the seller's numbers that had the company adding over a hundred new service sites with no additional staff and no additional vehicles. I wrote in the deck that it looked suspicious.

I still recommended pursuing the deal. The business was good and the constraint was a price and structure problem rather than a disqualifier. But I recommended it conditionally and put the question I needed answered in writing: what exactly the supplier agreement restricts, so we would know the real size of the opportunity. Recommending it and saying plainly what would change my mind seemed like the same job rather than two competing ones.

Signet Capital Advisors

Market Research Intern. July to August 2025.

A short remote engagement, two or three weeks, comparing two thermoplastics and injection molding companies in Ohio. I built three twelve-month financial statements for them and wrote up the industry: what drives demand, how the two were positioned differently, and which one I thought was the better business.

The more useful thing I got out of it was a mistake. I was offered the chance to do it in person in Cleveland and chose to stay remote because it seemed like a big step at the time. Nearly everything I have learned since came from being in a room with people who were better at this than me and watching how they worked. I don't turn those down anymore.

Those roles landed in a more useful order than I planned. Aegis was public markets: value a company you can only see from the outside. Liber was the buy side: decide whether to own something, with the seller's books in front of you. Batten was the sell side: represent a company and argue what it is worth to someone else. Doing one of each is most of why I am pointed at banking. What I want is to be close to the transaction and to have to defend a number to someone whose job is to attack it.

What I'm working on now

I've been following Ausgold, an Australian developer with a single project, Katanning, in Western Australia. OceanaGold agreed to buy it in August for about A$776 million. The deal hasn't closed and won't until December, subject to a shareholder vote.

Ausgold was already in a comparables model I built over the summer, so I had it valued before anyone was buying it. At A$1.36 a share the deal prices Katanning at about 0.75 times net asset value, against a median of roughly 0.42 times across the ten developers in my set. On enterprise value per resource ounce it's US$197 against a median of US$83. On the face of it OceanaGold paid up.

I don't think that's the right read. Trading multiples aren't takeover multiples, because a buyer taking control is pricing the deposit rather than the share price, and takeouts clear above where developers trade. The more interesting reason is in my own funding rows. Ausgold needed another US$187 million to build the mine, which meant issuing enough stock to dilute existing holders by around 58%. OceanaGold has US$655 million of cash and no debt. So the net asset value that the 0.75 multiple is measured against was never a number Ausgold's shareholders could have reached on their own. A multiple against a NAV the seller can't fund is not the same as a multiple against one the buyer can.

Where I'm stuck is that every feasibility study in my set assumes a gold price well below spot, Ausgold's by 26% and one peer by more than 50%. So the medians above aren't struck on a consistent gold price, which makes them softer than they look. I built a row to normalize all of them to a single screening price and it's filled in for six of the ten. Finishing that is the current job, and until it's done I hold the view loosely.

Separately, I'm going back through the returns sensitivity on the buyout model I built at the search fund, because I found errors in it. The internal rate of return moves the wrong way against entry price in one of the grids, which means something is wrong upstream of the output. It is less satisfying than finding a problem in someone else's numbers and it is the same job.
